What ships in the kit, what we may claim, and which regulatory frame applies all follow from that one question.

The clinical product

Clinical Intensive · $13,000

A licensed physical therapist delivers all 40 sessions and owns the PT plan of care. An MD owns medical diagnosis, the stool panel, supplements, and medical contraindications. Neither directs the other outside their license.

Why this structure holds

Licensed care, delivered by licensed people

Clinical Intensive makes treatment claims because a licensed clinician is actually delivering treatment. Every clinical act — ordering a lab, reading it, authoring a medical plan, establishing a PT plan of care, supervising a device — sits with someone whose license covers it.

What makes it defensible

  • A licensed PT delivers all 40 sessions. The prior model put an unlicensed concierge on screen for 68 hours while making clinical claims. That gap is closed.
  • Authority splits along scope lines. The MD owns medical diagnosis, labs, supplements, contraindications, and the medical plan. The PT independently evaluates, establishes and modifies the PT plan of care, and treats within PT scope.
  • The MD hour is costed, not assumed. v2.6.0 shipped labs with no clinician budgeted to order or interpret them.
  • Individualization is permitted precisely because a licensed clinician performs it — the same act that would be a violation in a wellness tier.

What still has to be true

  • PT licensure in each patient's state. A separate compact privilege is purchased per remote state, and the PT practices under the scope law of the state where the patient is located. The Compact does not cover every state; verify current status rather than relying on a count.
  • MD licensure and ordering authority in the state where the lab is ordered and the medical plan authored.
  • Clinical terminology is not efficacy substantiation. Describing services accurately is permitted; outcome claims remain an FTC question.
  • HIPAA coverage across every vendor touching PHI — signed BAAs, not assumed ones.
  • Telehealth consent and documentation per session, per state.

Design rationale, not a legal opinion. Nothing here has been reviewed by counsel. Every item in the right-hand column blocks go-live until it isn't.

Device review

One item cannot ship as described

ReviewSaeboStim · cranial placement. A PT license does not make every e-stim application permissible. Required before this ships again: exact device clearance and labeling, prescription status, pediatric intended use, listed contraindications, home-use labeling, and PT scope for this modality in the patient's state.

The specific concern: the protocol places pads on the face and behind the ears. General NMES clearances are typically for muscle re-education in the extremities, and labeling commonly contraindicates head and neck placement — which would make this company-directed off-label cranial use on a pediatric patient at home. This is arguably a larger exposure than the laser lane, because the laser has never been sold and this ships in the base kit today. If it resolves badly, SaeboStim leaves the kit and the hardware distinction between Clinical and Wellness narrows to nearly nothing.

The wellness product

NeuroHome Wellness · $5,000

A wellness coach guides a standardized routine three days a week for the first month, then twice a week for the second. No labs, no supplements, no MD, no estim. The coach may tailor for preference, order, and scheduling — never for a suspected deficit. That distinction is the whole product.

Why this structure holds

A general-wellness product, by construction

The product is built so that no clinical assessment, judgment, or treatment occurs in it — not by labeling, but by removing every element that would require them. FDA's general-wellness policy covers the low-risk devices used; the service itself sits under nonclinical coaching, state scope rules, and FTC advertising law.

What makes it defensible

  • Nothing diagnosis-driven exists in the product. No labs, no MD, no estim, no supplements in the plan. There is no clinical finding that could drive a change, because there is no clinical finding.
  • The coach has no clinical authority and no discretion to adapt for a suspected deficit. Personalization is limited to preference, order, and scheduling from an approved menu.
  • Claims stay on the wellness side — healthy routines, movement, coordination, relaxation, sleep habits.
  • Clinical add-ons are sold separately and never enter a session.

What still has to be true

  • The approved activity menu, coach training, and session logging. These make the boundary real and are not yet built or budgeted. The log must record the reason for a selection, so the record shows a non-clinical basis.
  • Device labeling and intended-use review. The light devices must be positioned for general wellness in their own labeling, not just in ours.
  • Marketing copy review. Intended use is evidenced by what is said publicly as much as in session. A single treatment claim on a landing page undoes the structure.
  • FTC substantiation for any objective health claim, however softly phrased.
  • A scripted escalation path for clinical questions, so the coach never improvises an answer.

Design rationale, not a legal opinion. Nothing here has been reviewed by counsel. The structure is only as good as the scripting and training that enforce it.

The coach boundary

What keeps this a wellness product

A coach may say

  • "Today's coordination activity is Cross Crawl."
  • "You said he's dragging today — let's start with the balance one instead."
  • "Today is the 10-minute general-wellness light routine."
  • "You logged four days this week. That's the rhythm we're after."
  • "That question is one for a clinician — let me get you the right conversation."

A coach may never say

  • "He's retained ATNR, so we need Cross Crawl."
  • "His balance looks weak — let's work on that."
  • "Let's increase the light dose."
  • "Skip that one today, his regulation is off."
  • Anything that changes the session because of a suspected deficit.

Future state · legal review required

Rover · not cleared for build.

A PT rotates between four Zoom rooms, providing synchronous skilled treatment to each child during every scheduled session. Between PT touchpoints the parent carries out previously taught home activities while a coach provides nonclinical logistical and documentation support. The economics are compelling. The model is not cleared.

Why this structure holds — and where it doesn't

Same licensed basis, new disclosure and privacy questions

Rover inherits Clinical Intensive's compliance basis — a licensed PT treats each child, the MD owns the medical decisions, the plan is individualized. What changes is that the clinician's attention is divided across four families. Four separate breakout rooms are not automatically equivalent to conventional group physical therapy.

What makes it defensible

  • A licensed PT provides synchronous skilled treatment to each child during every scheduled session. Group delivery by a licensed clinician is an established care model.
  • The MD role is unchanged — same hour, same ownership of the panel, the medical plan, and the supplement stack.
  • The support coach is scoped to logistics and documentation. No clinical authority, no direction.

What still has to be true

  • This creates disclosure, privacy, scope and supervision questions — and payer questions if billing is ever introduced. Counsel review is required before build, not before launch.
  • Privacy in a shared session. Whether any family can see or hear another family's session must be architecturally impossible, not procedurally discouraged.
  • Estim supervision. Estim must occur inside the PT touchpoint with the PT present in that family's room. The alternative is a separately established parent-home-use pathway. Neither exists — and SaeboStim is itself under device review.
  • Group delivery stated plainly in contract and marketing, judged on Rover's own copy.
  • PT licensure across every state in the pod, which may span four.
  • Coach scope in writing. The moment she directs treatment she is practicing.
  • Pod attrition terms in the contract, since delivery and refund both depend on pod composition.

Design rationale, not a legal opinion. Nothing here has been reviewed by counsel. The shared-room privacy question is new to this model and has no answer yet.

Two decisions before build

Allocation is not a promise

15 hours is an allocation, not a delivery guarantee. 60 ÷ 4 is how the cost divides, not proof any family received 15 hours. Converting it into a documented enforceable minimum makes the claim defensible — and removes the ability to give 25 hours to the dysregulated child and 8 to the easy one, which is the clinical reason to have a roving PT at all. Enforceable minimum and clinical flexibility are in direct tension. Choose deliberately, then write the marketing to match.

One clinical product at a time. Intensive and Rover both sit at $13,000 and are never co-listed, quoted, or compared. Switchover is a clean cutover — the rate card, the consult script, and the site show exactly one clinical product on any given day. What that policy does not solve: Rover's own claim language, cohort overlap in parent communities, repeat buyers who know what the old product felt like, and sales pitch drift. All four survive a rate-card change.

Sequencing. Run Intensive at launch — it works at family #1 and needs no cohort scheduling. Move to Rover when demand is dense enough that cohorts reliably fill at 3, not on a date. Below 2.55 rooms Rover is worse than Intensive; solo delivery lands 19.6 points under the product it replaces.
